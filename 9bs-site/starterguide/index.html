01 / BREW

Make your first espresso.

There is no machine-specific trick required to learn the basic workflow. Follow your manufacturer's setup instructions first, then use this as the coffee workflow.

01

Set up the machine

Wash removable parts, fill the water reservoir, perform the machine's initial rinse or priming procedure, and learn its controls. Use the manual for anything specific to your model.

02

Choose a baseline recipe

A useful espresso starting point is around a 1:2 brew ratio. For example, 18 g of coffee in and about 36 g of espresso out. The SCA reports an average practice around 18–20 g in, 36.5 g out and 25–30 seconds, but these are observations of practice, not a definition of good espresso.

Source: Specialty Coffee Association.

03

Prepare the coffee

Weigh your dose, grind it appropriately for espresso, distribute it evenly, and tamp the bed level. A consistent routine matters more than adding every accessory at once.

04

Brew by weight

Put the cup and scale under the outlet, start the shot, and stop at your target output. Record dose, output and time. These numbers make your next adjustment understandable.

05

Taste before changing anything

The goal is a cup you enjoy. Time is useful information, but a number on a timer is not proof that a shot is good.

06

Change one variable

Start with grind size for ordinary dialling-in. Keep dose and target output stable while you learn what the grind change does. Once the shot is broadly in range, taste and adjust your recipe deliberately.

Beginner rule: do not change the grinder, dose, yield, temperature, basket and tamping technique simultaneously. You will not know what fixed the problem.
What the numbers mean

Dose

The mass of dry coffee you put into the basket.

Yield

The mass of espresso you collect in the cup.

Ratio

Yield divided by dose. 18 g in and 36 g out is 1:2.

Time

How long the shot takes. Useful for diagnosing flow, but not a quality score.

Simple baseline

18 g → 36 g

Use this as an example, not a commandment. Your basket, coffee, grinder and taste may call for something different.

03 / LEARN

Understand what you are changing.

The deeper material belongs here, after you can make a repeatable drink.

01

Extraction

Brewing dissolves soluble material from coffee. More extraction is not automatically better. Evenness matters because uneven extraction can produce conflicting flavours in the same cup.

Read the Espresso Compass
02

Grind size

Changing grind size changes flow and extraction. Finer can increase extraction up to a point; grinding finer indefinitely can worsen unevenness and channeling.

Read about the limit
03

Freshness

Roasted coffee releases carbon dioxide rapidly after roasting, and grinding accelerates gas loss. Freshness is real, but there is no universal 7-day or 30-day rule that applies to every coffee.

SCA: Coffee Freshness
04

Channeling

Water follows paths of lower resistance. Uneven distribution or tamping can create preferential paths through the coffee bed, producing uneven extraction.

Barista Hustle: Channeling
05

Pressurised vs non-pressurised

Pressurised baskets create resistance through the basket outlet and can make brewing more forgiving. Non-pressurised baskets expose more of the brewing process to grind size, dose and puck preparation. Neither is automatically the right choice for every beginner.

06

Water

Water is not just a carrier. Its composition affects extraction and taste. Start with water your machine manufacturer permits and use a suitable treatment strategy if your local water or machine instructions call for one.

Important: Coffee advice often gets reduced to single-variable rules. Use them as hypotheses, then check the cup. If a coffee tastes good outside a conventional time or ratio, taste is the evidence that matters most for your drink.

05 / MILK

Steam milk by texture, not by a machine-specific trick.

Different machines have different steam power. The underlying sequence is similar.

01

Start cold

Use cold milk and a suitable pitcher. Begin with enough milk to cover the steam tip while leaving room for expansion.

02

Purge and position

Purge the wand according to the machine's instructions. Position the steam tip near the milk surface when you need to introduce air.

03

Stretch briefly

Introduce the amount of air needed for the drink. A latte generally needs less incorporated air than a cappuccino-style drink.

04

Create a whirlpool

Once enough air is incorporated, position the wand to create a rolling movement that distributes the bubbles through the milk.

05

Stop before overheating

Around 60–65°C is a practical working range for many milk drinks, but use your taste, texture and machine's guidance rather than treating a single temperature as a law.

06

Clean immediately

Purge and wipe the wand immediately after steaming, following the machine manufacturer's cleaning instructions.

For a practical milk-steaming reference, see KitchenAid's milk-steaming guide. Machine-specific instructions take precedence.

06 / MAINTAIN

Cleaning is part of the workflow.

Maintenance instructions vary by machine. The manufacturer manual always takes precedence over a generic coffee guide.

After brewing

Keep the brew path clean

Knock out the puck, rinse the basket and portafilter as appropriate, and flush the group according to your machine's instructions.

After steaming

Clean the steam wand

Purge and wipe the wand immediately so milk residue does not dry inside or on the steam tip.

Regularly

Follow the manual

Clean removable components and internal parts at the frequency specified by your machine. Do not invent a descaling schedule that conflicts with the manufacturer.

When prompted

Descale correctly

Descaling frequency depends on machine design, water conditions and usage. Use the manufacturer's stated procedure and product guidance.

Safety and warranty: Modifications and cleaning chemicals are not generic coffee advice. Use the machine manual and manufacturer's instructions before experimenting.
