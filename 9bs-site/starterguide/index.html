01 / BREW

Your first espresso.

Use this as a starting workflow, not a recipe carved in stone. Coffee, equipment and taste vary.

Starting point: a roughly 1:2 brew ratio is a useful place to begin. SCA research on contemporary espresso practice found many baristas using around 18–20 g of coffee and roughly 36.5 g of beverage, with times around 25–30 seconds. That describes observed practice, not a definition of good espresso.
01

Choose a sensible dose

Start near the middle of the dose range your basket is designed for. Do not assume every “double” basket takes 18 g.

Check the basket specification or manufacturer guidance when available.

02

Weigh the coffee

Record the dry dose. This gives you a stable starting variable when you change grind or yield.

03

Grind for espresso

Use an espresso-capable grinder and choose a setting that gives the machine reasonable resistance. There is no universal grinder setting.

04

Distribute the grounds

Break up obvious clumps and distribute the coffee evenly across the basket. The goal is a repeatable, even puck, not a particular ritual.

05

Tamp level

Compress the bed evenly and keep the tamper level. Consistency matters more than chasing a particular tamping-force number.

06

Measure the output

Put the cup on a scale, start the timer when you start brewing, and stop around your chosen beverage mass. Record the result.

07

Taste before changing anything

Ask whether it is sweet, balanced, sharp, bitter, dry, thin, heavy or otherwise unpleasant. Taste is the primary feedback signal.

Concept

Dose

The mass of dry coffee entering the basket. Changing dose changes the amount of coffee and can also change puck depth and flow resistance.

Concept

Yield

The mass of brewed beverage in the cup. Brew ratio is yield divided by dose.

Concept

Time

Useful for describing and reproducing a shot, but not a quality score. Two shots with the same time can taste very different.

Workflow

Change one variable

Keep dose and target yield stable while testing grind, then taste. Once the result is sensible, explore yield or other variables deliberately.

FreshnessUseful context, not a countdown timer
What the SCA says: whole-bean coffee can reasonably be considered fresh for roughly three weeks after roasting when using CO₂ degassing as a guide, while ground coffee loses CO₂ much faster. That does not mean every coffee tastes best on the same day.

Buy by roast date where available, store beans appropriately, and let the coffee tell you when it works well. If a coffee is difficult to extract, do not assume age is the only cause.

02 / FIX

Fix the shot you actually pulled.

These are diagnostic directions, not guarantees. Extraction problems, uneven extraction, roast character and grinder limitations can overlap.

Why the simple chart has limitsExtraction is not one-dimensional
Espresso

Yield changes strength and extraction

Increasing yield generally makes espresso weaker while also increasing extraction. Reducing yield does the opposite.

Evenness

Uneven extraction can hide inside “sour” or “bitter”

Barista Hustle notes that sour and bitter flavours can coexist when extraction is uneven. Improving distribution can enlarge the range of good results.

Channeling

Water takes the easier path

Uneven density in the puck can create preferential flow. Distribution and tamping are therefore part of extraction, not decoration.

Limits

Finer is not infinitely better

Grinding finer can increase extraction until uneven flow becomes a problem. Beyond that point, extraction can decline rather than continue upward.

03 / LEARN

Understand what is happening in the puck.

You do not need to memorise coffee science before making coffee. These are the concepts that make troubleshooting easier.

01

Extraction

Brewing dissolves compounds from ground coffee. The goal is not “maximum extraction”; it is a cup whose extracted flavours work together.

Espresso Compass →
02

Strength

Strength describes how concentrated the beverage is. Brew ratio is one of the major controls, which is why yield changes can alter both strength and extraction.

Read the Compass →
03

Grind size

Grind changes the surface area available to water and changes resistance through the coffee bed. The useful setting is the one that produces the cup you want.

04

Channeling

Water can move preferentially through less-dense regions. Severe channeling can produce weak, unbalanced espresso even when the shot looks fast enough on paper.

Barista Hustle: channeling →
05

Freshness

Roasted coffee releases gases over time. Grinding accelerates the loss of CO₂ dramatically, which is one reason freshly ground whole beans are useful for espresso.

SCA: coffee freshness →
06

Pressurised baskets

A pressurised basket creates additional resistance and can make less-controlled coffee easier to brew. A non-pressurised basket makes grind and puck preparation much more consequential.

How to use referencesLearn, then test
Start with the practical workflow. Make a shot before diving into extraction theory.
Use sources to understand a variable. Then change one thing on your own setup.
Do not turn an observed practice into a law. Even SCA's espresso research describes what surveyed baristas were doing rather than defining one universally correct recipe.

04 / EQUIPMENT

Buy the thing that solves the problem.

The original 9BS source had a useful equipment catalogue. We are preserving the categories and community leads while removing old prices, rankings and product claims until they are individually verified.

STAGE 1

Grinder

High impact

A capable grinder gives you control over flow. If your grinder cannot produce a repeatable espresso grind, accessories will not fix that limitation.

STAGE 2

Scale

High impact

Measure dose and beverage mass. A timer makes the workflow easier to reproduce and compare.

STAGE 3

Distribution

Useful

WDT or another repeatable distribution method can help break clumps and distribute grounds. It is a tool, not a magic extraction button.

STAGE 4

Basket

Setup dependent

Move from a pressurised to a non-pressurised basket when your grinder and workflow are ready for the added control.

STAGE 5

Bottomless portafilter

Diagnostic

It exposes puck-flow problems visually. It does not itself make espresso taste better.

STAGE 6

Everything else

Optional

Dosing funnels, puck screens, knock boxes and storage systems can improve workflow or cleanliness. Buy them because they solve a specific annoyance.

Accessory guideWhat each item is actually for
WDT tool: distributes and de-clumps grounds. Useful when it makes your preparation more repeatable.
RDT: a small amount of water can reduce static during grinding. Use only an appropriate amount and keep moisture away from the grinder's internal electronics.
Puck screen: a thin screen placed above the puck. It can change headspace and cleanup, so fit and dose need to be considered.
Dosing funnel: reduces grounds spilling while dosing and distributing.
Tamper: creates a level, compressed coffee bed. Fit and level matter more than marketing around force.
Knock box: convenience and cleanup, not an extraction upgrade.

05 / COFFEE

Choose beans by the cup you want.

Roast level, processing, origin, freshness and your recipe all affect the result. “Medium” is a useful starting description, not a universal guarantee.

Starting point

Medium to medium-dark

These can be a forgiving starting range for people learning espresso, especially if they prefer chocolate, nutty or caramelised flavours.

Experiment

Lighter roasts

They can require a different recipe and may highlight acidity or fruit more strongly. Do not diagnose every bright flavour as a bad extraction.

Freshness

Use roast date where possible

Freshness affects aroma and extraction behaviour. Store beans consistently and compare the cup rather than following a rigid calendar.

Milk

Match the coffee to the drink

A coffee that is subtle as straight espresso may disappear in a large milk drink. That is a recipe and preference question, not a quality ranking.

06 / MILK

Steam for texture, not a temperature number.

The same basic sequence works across many steam wands. Machine power changes the timing, but the goal remains controlled air incorporation followed by a smooth whirlpool.

01

Start cold

Use cold milk and a suitable pitcher. Cold milk gives you more time to incorporate air and build texture.

02

Purge the wand

Clear condensation and follow the machine's steam-start procedure.

03

Introduce air briefly

Keep the tip near the surface when you want to introduce air. The sound should be controlled rather than aggressive.

04

Create a whirlpool

Once enough air is incorporated, position the wand so the milk rolls. This distributes the bubbles through the liquid.

05

Stop before overheating

Around 60–65°C is a practical working range for many milk drinks, but use texture and taste rather than treating one temperature as a law.

06

Clean immediately

Purge and wipe the wand immediately after steaming, following the machine manufacturer's cleaning instructions.

Milk drink differencesTexture changes more than the coffee does

Latte

Generally smoother and less aerated than a traditional cappuccino. Exact ratios vary.

Cappuccino

More aeration and a thicker foam layer than a latte, though modern café definitions vary.

Flat white

Typically a smaller drink with fine microfoam and a stronger coffee-to-milk relationship.

Practice

Focus on consistent pitcher position, air injection and rolling motion before worrying about latte art.

07 / MAINTAIN

Cleaning is part of the workflow.

Maintenance differs by machine. The manufacturer manual always takes precedence over a generic coffee guide.

After brewing

Keep the brew path clean

Knock out the puck, rinse the basket and portafilter as appropriate, and flush the group according to the machine's instructions.

After steaming

Clean the steam wand

Purge and wipe the wand immediately so milk residue does not dry on or inside the steam tip.

Regularly

Clean removable parts

Follow the machine's cleaning schedule and instructions for baskets, portafilters, trays, tanks and other removable parts.

When prompted

Descale correctly

Descaling frequency depends on machine design, water conditions and usage. Use the manufacturer's stated procedure and product guidance.

Do not generalise machine maintenance. The original site contained detailed Dedica procedures. Those are now classified as machine-specific and should return only in a separately verified machine guide.
