02 The three most important components

Prioritise these three components. In most setups, the grinder deserves at least as much attention as the espresso machine.

#1

Fresh beans

The single biggest lever. Stale supermarket beans = sad espresso no matter what. Buy freshly roasted, rest 7–10 days off roast date, use within a month.

See bean picks →
#2

A real grinder

The grinder has a major effect on extraction quality. A capable grinder gives you consistent particle size and makes dialing in substantially easier.

Find your grinder →
#3

A non-pressurised basket

The main upgrade is moving from a pressurised basket to a non-pressurised basket. A precision IMS basket is a useful option, and a bottomless portafilter makes channeling easier to identify. A purpose-built bottomless portafilter is simpler than modifying the stock portafilter.

Baskets & portafilters →

Keep the stock pressurised basket or portafilter if you use pre-ground coffee or want a more forgiving workflow.

03 Choosing an espresso grinder

Espresso requires a fine, consistent, espresso-capable grind. A grinder designed only for filter coffee may make dialing in difficult. Filter the options by budget and type.

Choose an electric grinder if your budget allows. Manual grinders such as the K6 and C3 ESP can produce good espresso, but electric grinders are slower for daily use.

04 Choosing coffee beans in India

Baskets

The basket matters more than the portafilter body. A precision basket (IMS-grade) gives more even extraction. But honestly: a decent grinder + fresh beans + a good-enough basket beats a fancy basket with bad grind.

    Never put a pressurised basket in a bottomless portafilter — you get foam, not crema, spraying everywhere.

    06 Dialing in espresso

    Stop trusting the buttons (the "2x" button pulls a lungo). Get a scale, weigh everything, change one variable at a time.

    ▶ Recommended dialing-in tutorial A practical introduction to dialing in espresso, covering grind size, dose, yield and extraction time. — youtube.com ↗

    The starting point

    Dose inShot outTimeRatio
    Double18 g36 g25–32 s1:2
    Single9 g~22 g25–30 s1:2.5
    Light roast18 g45–54 glonger1:2.5–1:3

    Timer starts the moment you press the button (coffee-water contact), not at first drip.

    1. Weigh your dose. ~17–18g fills a stock double basket nicely (with a puck screen).
    2. RDT: one spritz of water on the beans to kill static.
    3. Grind straight into the basket.
    4. WDT: stir with a thin needle to break clumps & distribute.
    5. Level & tamp until it stops moving. (You basically can't tamp "too hard" — you'd need a hydraulic press.)
    6. Puck screen on top, lock in, and pull onto a scale.
    7. Taste & adjust → see "Espresso troubleshooting" below.
    Run a hotter shot — the high-temp trick from Tom's Coffee Corner. The espresso machine's little thermoblock cools down between heating and brewing, so by default your shot brews cooler than ideal. The fix the group swears by: right before you pull, run a short blank flush of hot water through the empty group head to pre-heat it, then lock in your portafilter and brew immediately. Even better — do back-to-back flushes so the boiler stays hot, and pull the moment it's ready. Your shot runs noticeably hotter and extracts sweeter, which unlocks lighter roasts the espresso machine otherwise struggles with. Watch the trick ↗ (and a second take ↗).
    Pull a "salami shot" to train your palate. Put 3–4 small cups under a (bottomless) shot and swap them every few seconds as it pours. Taste each in order: the first pours sharp & sour (the acids come out first), the middle is sweet & balanced, and the last turns thin & bitter. Doing this once teaches you what "sour vs bitter" actually tastes like — so the "Espresso troubleshooting" advice below finally clicks.

    08 Steaming milk with a home espresso machine

    Steam power varies between machines. Technique, milk temperature and wand positioning have a greater effect on the result than power alone.

    ▶ Milk steaming, demoed for this machine A practical milk-steaming demonstration covering wand position, air injection and creating a stable whirlpool. — youtube.com ↗
    1. Purge first. Run the wand 20–30s until you get real steam pressure. 5 seconds is not enough — "an un-purged wand is useless."
    2. Cold milk, right jug. Chill the milk (and the pitcher in the freezer). Lower-fat tetra milk foams easier for beginners — Amul Blue or Akshayakalpa are frequently useds.
    3. Stretch: tip just at the surface → listen for a soft "tsss… tsss" as you inject air for ~3–5s.
    4. Texture: submerge slightly to create a whirlpool and heat through, no more hissing.
    5. Stop at ~60–65°C. Above 65°C milk proteins break and it'll never be silky again (and it scalds).
    Cappuccino = more foam · Latte / flat white = less, silkier.
    Start with roughly 100–140g of cold milk and adjust the amount to suit the drink and pitcher.
    Purge & wipe the wand immediately after every steam or it clogs.
    Practise for free with dish soap. Fill the pitcher with water + a drop of dishwashing liquid and steam that — it stretches and textures just like milk, so you can drill your wand technique and latte-art pours without wasting milk or coffee. Rinse well after.
